# Medical practices
- Demand is residents x the share whose insurance you take x how often each age group uses the specialty.
- Referral flows decide many specialties: primary care -> specialists; urgent care and ERs -> fracture and injury care;
  surgeons -> physical therapy; schools, employers and occupational health -> sports and work injuries.
- Hospitals play two roles: (1) physicians operate and round there, so drive time from clinic to the hospital where
  they have privileges is a daily cost and a reason to cluster nearby; (2) hospital systems employ competing
  physicians and run their own outpatient sites that capture patients. Treat them as separate factors with
  opposite signs when both apply.
- Payer mix by neighborhood (Medicare, Medicaid/CHIP, commercial, uninsured) changes revenue per visit.
- Access: older or injured patients need parking near the door, step-free entry, drop-off lanes; Medicaid families
  need bus lines; long waits for rides cause no-shows.
- Visibility matters for walk-in care (urgent care, retail clinics) and almost not at all for referral or
  appointment-based care (surgery centers, physical therapy, imaging).
- Service lines change the site: imaging and surgery need ground-floor, heavy-load, shielded or certified space; a
  medical office building near a hospital simplifies credentialing and call coverage.
- Medical office space near the Texas Medical Center is costly and congested; suburban growth corridors (Katy,
  Cypress, The Woodlands, Sugar Land, Pearland, Kingwood) are where new households and young families are.
