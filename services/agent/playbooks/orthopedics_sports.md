# Sports medicine
Who gets hurt: high school athletes (football, soccer, basketball, volleyball, baseball, track), college and club
athletes, and active adults 18-44 (running, CrossFit, pickleball, recreational leagues). ACL and meniscus tears,
shoulder instability, ankle sprains and fractures, and concussions are the core case mix; ACL reconstruction and
arthroscopy are the surgical pull-through, so commercial insurance dominates and Medicaid/CHIP matter for teens.
How they reach a clinic: the school's athletic trainer (ATC) evaluates the injury on the field and sends the athlete
to the physician who covers that school, usually the next morning. Texas UIL 5A/6A schools (about 1,300+ students)
field the biggest programs and usually have full-time athletic trainers; 4A and smaller schools often share one.
Sideline coverage contracts follow relationships, not proximity alone, but a clinic 30+ minutes from a school loses
the Saturday-morning injury visits to whoever is closer.
Timing: Friday-night football and weekend tournaments mean Saturday-morning injury clinics and Monday rush; after-
school slots (3:30-6 pm) for students; season peaks in fall (football) and spring (soccer, baseball).
Site: X-ray on site is mandatory (same-visit diagnosis), a casting and bracing room, space for a sports PT gym or a
PT partner close by (return-to-play rehab 2-3 times a week), parking for parents dropping off teens.
Competition: hospital system sports-medicine programs that sponsor school districts (naming rights, ATC staffing
contracts) can lock in a district's referrals; orthopedic urgent cares capture walk-in sprains.
Must keep:
- `high school|athlet|uil|student` High schools with large athletic programs (UIL 5A/6A enrollment) within a short drive
- `youth|teen|18-44|active adult|club sport|sports complex|field` Young and active population: teens and adults 18-44, club sports complexes
- `physical therap|\bpt\b|rehab|return-to-play` Physical therapy nearby for return-to-play rehab
